# Domain 3: Food, Water & Land Systems

## Research Summary (October 2026)

- SOFI 2026 reports **7.8% of the world undernourished in 2025** and **2.1 billion people experiencing moderate or severe food insecurity**. In Africa, about two-thirds of people cannot afford a healthy diet. The 2026 Global Report on Food Crises says acute hunger has doubled over the past decade.
- Food loss and waste remains roughly **13.2% lost after harvest and before retail, plus 19% wasted** at retail, food service and household level. It generates an estimated **8–10% of global greenhouse-gas emissions**.
- FAO's *Status of the World's Soil Resources 2026* finds **58% of regional assessments show deterioration since 2015**, with only about 10% improving. About 40% of global land is degraded, affecting 3.2 billion people. Every dollar invested in restoration returns an estimated US$7–30.
- Water problems are rarely "no water" in aggregate. They are leakage, contamination, allocation, maintenance and the absence of a responsible institution. Rural piped schemes and peri-urban wastewater reuse are two of the least instrumented seams.
- Livestock and aquaculture are growing sources of protein and income, but disease, heat stress and antibiotic misuse are managed with little measurement at the farm gate.
- The 12 problems below avoid another crop-advice app or another cold-storage box. They attack **measurement at the point of loss**, **trust infrastructure for sharing**, and **the last mile of food safety**.

## Axioms and Aristotelian Frame

**Material cause:** water, soil, seed, feed, fish, milk, heat, microbes and time. Biological systems degrade on their own timescales; a delay of hours can destroy value built over months.

**Formal cause:** supply-chain contracts, insurance rules, land records, water rights, cooperative ownership, and the separation of testing from buying.

**Efficient cause:** milk collection centres, farmer producer organisations, water committees, fisher cooperatives, municipal utilities, insurers, and the workers who sample and inspect.

**Final cause:** safe, affordable food; water that reaches the tap; secure land; and farmers and fishers who keep more of the value they create.

**Axiom list:**

1. Biological value decays exponentially with time and temperature.
2. Testing at the point of exchange changes behaviour more than testing in a distant lab.
3. Smallholders adopt practices when the buyer, not the brochure, requires them.
4. Water leaks and contamination are invisible until measured; maintenance is the actualising condition.
5. Sharing fails without a trust protocol (deposit, adjudication, insurance), not because people are selfish.
6. Land disputes are about evidence and relationships, not only law.
7. Heat stress and disease are measurable before yield collapses.
8. Surplus food is a logistics and safety problem, not a scarcity problem.

**Derivation:** A1 + A2 → put the test at the collection centre, not the lab. A3 → design the intervention around the buyer's acceptance rule. A4 → instrument the scheme and name the maintainer. A5 → sharing needs adjudication and insurance. A7 → early warning converts loss into a decision.

## Portfolio Logic

The portfolio follows the biological clock: rows 1, 7, 10 and 11 act before loss; rows 2, 3, 5 and 8 fix the evidence and trust that determine whether a farmer or fisher can insure, share or get paid; rows 4, 6, 9 and 12 fix the seams between systems (wastewater and farming, lab and pond, court and village, event and hungry household).

What is excluded: another crop-disease classifier, another seed-authenticity blockchain, another generic cold-storage box, and another farm-to-consumer marketplace. Those are crowded, and most do not fix the point-of-loss measurement or the trust protocol.

## Kill Criteria and Review Notes

- Each row has a measurable loss, contamination or dispute outcome, not just adoption.
- The hard dependencies are buyer acceptance (rows 1, 4, 7), an insurer (row 2), a hydrogeology partner (row 5), a lab (row 6), and a legal partner (row 9).
- The "who pays" answer is usually the buyer, insurer, utility, cooperative or funder whose existing cost is the loss. Do not rely on farmer willingness to pay alone.
- The ethics guardrail: no shaming of farmers or fishers; no unsafe food redistribution; no surveillance of informal workers; publish methods and failures.
