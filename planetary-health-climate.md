# Domain 1: Planetary Health & Climate Adaptation

## Research Summary (October 2026)

- The WEF *Global Risks Report 2026* puts extreme weather as the top long-term risk and says half of the ten most severe risks over the next decade are environmental. The near-term picture is dominated by geoeconomic confrontation, misinformation and polarisation, which makes adaptation finance and local trust the binding constraints.
- WHO's *World Health Statistics 2026* attributes about **6.6 million deaths in 2021** to household and ambient air pollution. Heat, wildfire smoke, floods and mould are not separate "environmental" issues; they are direct determinants of respiratory and cardiovascular health.
- WWF's *Living Planet Report 2026* finds a **73% average decline in monitored vertebrate populations** between 1970 and 2022, with freshwater populations down 85%. Half of monitored populations are stable or increasing, which means recovery is possible where the pressure is removed.
- FAO's *Status of the World's Soil Resources 2026* reports that **58% of regional assessments show soil deterioration since 2015**, with only about 10% improving. About 40% of global land is degraded, affecting 3.2 billion people.
- Food loss and waste remains roughly **13.2% lost + 19% wasted**, generating 8–10% of global greenhouse-gas emissions. The adaptation agenda is therefore also a materials, cold-chain and maintenance agenda.
- The adaptation gap is rarely a lack of knowledge. It is a lack of **maintenance, verification, local trust, and someone whose budget pays for prevention**. Every problem below is chosen because a 1–3 person team can change one of those actualising conditions.

## Axioms and Aristotelian Frame

**Material cause:** heat, water, particulate matter, soil, habitat, and the physical built environment. These obey thermodynamics and biology; they do not respond to messaging.

**Formal cause:** split incentives (landlord vs tenant, planter vs maintainer, polluter vs downstream), missing measurement, and emergency budgets that fund response but not prevention.

**Efficient cause:** municipal engineers, landlords, forest guards, farmers, insurers, community health workers, and the small teams who can give them a cheap measurement or a standard operating procedure.

**Final cause:** the telos is not "climate resilience" in the abstract. It is a child sleeping in a safe temperature, a household that does not lose its home to mould, a farmer who knows when to switch crops, and a city that plants trees it can keep alive.

**Axiom list:**

1. Heat and pollutants follow physical laws; exposure is cumulative and often silent.
2. Prevention is cheaper than response, but prevention budgets are politically weaker.
3. Split incentives reproduce neglect: the person who owns the asset is not the person who bears the harm.
4. What is not measured is not maintained.
5. Trust is local; a known person outweighs a distant institution.
6. Ecosystems recover when pressure is removed, but recovery time is measured in decades.
7. Every adaptation intervention has a maintenance cost; ignoring it converts a solution into a future failure.
8. Behaviour follows defaults, friction, and visible peer behaviour more than information.

**Derivation:** A1 + A2 → harm accumulates while prevention is underfunded. A3 → the owner of the shell, road, drain, or field does not bear the full cost of neglect. A4 → cheap measurement plus a named responsible person changes behaviour. A5 → adoption runs through trusted local messengers. A7 → design for maintenance or do not deploy.

## Portfolio Logic

These 12 are a portfolio, not a list. Rows 1, 3, 4 and 9 attack the same telos (safe shelter) through four different efficient causes: the lease, the first 72 hours, the public refuge, and the activation network. Rows 2, 5, 6 and 8 attack the maintenance and verification gap in nature. Rows 7, 10, 11 and 12 are the slow-infrastructure bets: salinity, debris, heat income and street-level data.

What is deliberately excluded: carbon-credit marketplaces, generic climate dashboards, tree-planting apps, and another air-quality monitor. Those are crowded, and most fail the "who pays for maintenance" test.

## Kill Criteria and Review Notes

- Every row has a measurable first-version result and a stop rule.
- The common failure mode is a pilot that works technically but has no institution to pay for maintenance. Each row names the payer or the budget line to be tested.
- Rows 1, 2, 3 and 9 require a field partner (municipal ward, landlord association, relief agency). Do not start without one.
- Rows 5, 6, 10 and 11 require a regulator, insurer or procurement partner. The first version should be a jointly authored protocol, not a finished app.
- The ethics guardrail is simple: no surveillance of tenants, workers or households; no exaggerated survival or health claims; publish failures.
